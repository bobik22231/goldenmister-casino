How the peg pyramid works

The board is built as a triangle of staggered pegs, with each row one peg wider than the last. A ball dropped at the top has an even chance of moving left or right at every peg, then repeats that split again and again until it reaches the base. The final stop is one of the pockets along the bottom, which are always one more than the number of rows.

Because every turn is a fresh fifty-fifty deflection, balls gather near the centre and only rarely travel all the way to an outer edge. Statisticians call this a Galton board, and the curve it creates is a binomial distribution. The payout table simply reverses that curve: the centre is common and usually pays less, while the edges are harder to hit and usually pay more.

Straight answers to five questions

Is every drop genuinely random?

On licensed sites, yes. Each deflection comes from an audited random number generator, and provably fair setups let you check afterwards that the path was fixed before the ball moved.

Do more rows make the game pay better?

No. Extra rows add more pockets and stretch the odds curve, so edge prizes become larger while their chance becomes smaller. The overall return stays about the same across board sizes.

Which risk level is the smart choice?

No setting is mathematically better. Low risk smooths the ride, while high risk concentrates value in rare edge hits. Choose according to how much swing your bankroll can handle.
